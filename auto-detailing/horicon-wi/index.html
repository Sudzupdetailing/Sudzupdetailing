Auto Detailing for Horicon, Wisconsin

Horicon sits on the edge of the largest freshwater cattail marsh in the United States, and living next to a marsh does things to a vehicle that living next to a lake does not.

Horicon Marsh

Marsh air

A marsh is not a lake. It is thousands of acres of shallow water and decaying vegetation, and it produces a humidity and an organic load in the air that the surrounding town lives in.

For vehicles that means two things. Paint stays damp longer into the morning than it does a few miles away on dry ground, which extends the window in which whatever landed overnight can bond. And the dust that settles on a Horicon vehicle in late summer is partly organic — fine plant material off the marsh — which stains as it decomposes on a wet surface rather than simply sitting there.

Mosquito season is the other marsh contribution, and the residue from a summer of windscreen and bonnet impacts is protein that etches if it bakes.

Migration season traffic

Spring and autumn bring waterfowl migrations through the marsh on a scale that draws birders and photographers from across the region, and Horicon's roads carry them.

For local vehicles that is slow traffic on marsh-edge roads that flood at the margins, mud tracked in from the viewing areas and the trail heads, and a lot of stop-start driving that never fully warms a cabin in the shoulder seasons.

Marsh mud is its own material

Marsh mud is fine, black, organic and wet, and it behaves differently from field mud or trail mud. It works into carpet fibre while still wet and it continues to decompose there, which produces a smell that people in the town often stop noticing.

It needs extraction rather than vacuuming, and on a vehicle that visits the marsh regularly it needs it more than once a year.

A manufacturing town on the marsh

Horicon is a company town in the old sense: the John Deere works on the south side is one of the largest employers in the county and it has been there for well over a century. That puts shift vehicles on Horicon roads at a scale the size of the city does not suggest, and a shift vehicle has a specific life — eight to twelve hours parked outside a plant taking a full dew cycle every day, driven home tired, and the only place its owner eats some days.

The paint on those vehicles takes industrial fallout on top of the marsh humidity: metallic particulate lands on the bonnet and roof, embeds and corrodes in place, and the damp air off the marsh gives it more time to bond than it would get anywhere drier. It shows on light paint as rust-coloured specks, and chemical iron removal is what dissolves it.

The Rock River through town

The Rock River leaves the marsh at Horicon and runs through the middle of the city, and the low ground along it holds humidity into the mornings the way river corridors do everywhere. Vehicles parked near it spend more time wet, and in a bad spring the river rises — a car in one of the low lots during a rise has water in its padding whether the carpet feels wet a week later or not.

Riverside parking also means tree cover, and the sap, droppings and leaf tannin that come with it on paint that is already damp.

Winter on the marsh

The marsh freezes and the wind off it in January is colder and wetter than wind off farmland. Roads along the marsh edge ice earlier, stay iced longer and get more plough runs than the county average, so Horicon vehicles take more brine per mile than their neighbours in Mayville or Juneau. Vehicles that park facing the marsh take frost on the windward side every morning.

That makes the winter cabin problem worse here than almost anywhere we serve. Short trips through town never warm a cabin, marsh air keeps everything damp, and road salt in the carpet backing holds the moisture. January is the month for interior work in Horicon, because that is when the salt is in the carpet and the smell is establishing rather than established.

The Horicon calendar

January for the cabin. April for the full reset, with iron removal for the plant fallout and extraction for a season of marsh damp. May and September for the migration seasons — not for detailing, but for knowing that the vehicle is about to do the dikes and the viewing areas and will need the marsh mud dealt with afterward. September for protection before the next winter, and specifically a coating on a vehicle that parks near the marsh or the plant, because dwell time is the whole problem and a coating is the only thing that shortens it.
