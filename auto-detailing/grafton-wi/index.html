What a parking lot does to a car

The retail development along the highway corridor means Grafton vehicles spend a lot of time in large surface parking lots, and lots are harder on paint and trim than people account for.

Door dings and cart contact are the obvious ones. Less obvious is lot sealant. Large lots get seal-coated regularly, and fresh sealant transfers onto tyres, then onto rocker panels and wheel arches as a tar-like film that washing does not remove. Lot drainage also concentrates runoff — oil, coolant and brake dust from hundreds of vehicles — into the low spots where cars park, and that film gets thrown onto lower panels by every tyre.

Retail lots are also where vehicles sit stationary in full sun for hours, which is where dash and leather UV damage accumulates.

I-43 commuting and the river

The village sits on I-43 with most local commuting running south on it, which means interstate brine exposure in winter across the whole vehicle.

The Milwaukee River runs through the historic downtown and the low ground near it holds humidity, extending dew time on paint and keeping cabin carpet damp longer in winter.

Two very different Graftons

The historic village core along the river and the modern retail corridor along the highway produce different vehicles. Village vehicles do short damp trips; corridor vehicles do interstate miles and long stationary hours in lots. A Grafton car that does both takes the worst of each.

The mill district and the river walk

Grafton's historic core sits on the Milwaukee River around the old mill, and it drives differently from the corridor. Streets are narrow, parking is on-street or in small municipal lots under trees, and the trips are short — a few blocks to the shops, the bakery, the river walk. Short trips are the ones that never warm a cabin in winter, so moisture that came in on shoes stays in the carpet, and the river holds humidity in the low ground that keeps it there.

Tree-lined street parking adds sap, droppings and tannin to the paint, and on a car that spends its evenings downtown the frequency is daily. On a light-coloured car, the tannin outline of a wet oak leaf left over a weekend is the mark we most often get asked about from the village side of Grafton.

The mill district also floods at the margins in a bad spring. A car parked in one of the low lots along the river during a rise has water in its padding whether or not the carpet feels wet a week later, and that is the one case where waiting to see if a smell develops is the wrong call.

The Interurban Trail and the outdoor side

The Ozaukee Interurban Trail runs through Grafton, and the village is a jumping-on point for a lot of cyclists and walkers from across the county. That puts bike racks on Grafton cars — hitch racks that swing against tailgates, roof racks that leave drip lines — and trail grit and chain lube on rear bumpers.

It also puts a lot of dogs in Grafton vehicles. The trail is a dog-walking corridor, and a wet dog after a river-edge walk gets into the cargo area and lies down. The moisture goes into the carpet backing, and the bacteria that follow are the smell that comes back on humid days. Fabric protection on the cargo area before the season is what keeps that from starting.

What we see most from Grafton, and when

Three patterns account for most Grafton vehicles we work on. The corridor commuter, with sealant film on the lower panels from the retail lots, I-43 brine across the whole car, and a dash that has taken hundreds of hours of lot sun. The village car, with tree residue on the paint and a cabin that stays damp from short river-side trips. And the outdoor vehicle, with rack marks, trail grit and a dog in the back.

The timing for all three is the same as everywhere in Ozaukee County: April for the reset once I-43 stops being treated, and September for protection before it starts again. The exception is a car that was in a flooded lot, which needs to be looked at inside a couple of weeks regardless of the month.
