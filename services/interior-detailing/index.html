Interior Detailing

The vacuum is not the quick part

Most of the time on an interior detail goes into vacuuming, and most of that goes into places a normal vacuum has never reached.

Seats get moved fully forward and fully back. The carpet under the seat rails collects more debris than any other surface in a vehicle and it is the single most commonly skipped area, because reaching it means moving seats twice.

Floor mats come out and get done separately from the carpet underneath. That carpet is frequently dirtier than the mat that was covering it, which surprises people every time.

Cargo areas get the load floor lifted and the spare wheel well cleared. On SUVs and estates that space collects an extraordinary amount of grit and it is almost never touched by anyone.

Compressed air comes first

This is the step that separates a detail from a thorough clean, and it happens before the vacuum rather than after.

A vacuum works by moving air across a surface. Inside a three-millimetre gap — a seat seam, a belt buckle recess, the channel where the console meets the carpet, a vent louvre — there is no airflow to move. The nozzle sits over the gap and nothing happens. You can vacuum a seam for a minute and remove nothing from inside it.

Compressed air solves it by working the other direction: blow the debris out into the open cabin, then collect it. It is loud, it is messy, and it is the reason a detailed interior stays clean longer than a vacuumed one. Debris left in seams migrates back out over the following weeks, which is why a rushed clean looks like it did not last.

Extraction, and why surface cleaning fails

Carpet and cloth upholstery are not surfaces, they are depth. The visible fibres are the top of a structure that goes down to a backing layer, and almost everything that matters — salt, spilled liquid, embedded dirt, moisture — lives below the part you can see.

Hot water extraction injects solution into that depth and pulls it back out under vacuum along with whatever it has dissolved. Wiping, scrubbing or spraying works the top layer only, which is why a stain treated on the surface reappears a week later. It did not come back. It wicked upward from the backing as the carpet dried, which is exactly what it was always going to do.

In Wisconsin this matters more than it does most places, because what is in the backing is largely road salt. Salt is hygroscopic — it draws moisture in — so it keeps a carpet damp, which is what produces the smell people notice in February and blame on a spill they cannot find.

Surface treatment, and why we finish matte

Vinyl, rubber and plastic treatment is the step people misunderstand most. It is not a shine product. Its job is to clean hard surfaces properly and then replace protection on plastics that lose plasticiser to UV over time.

Untreated dashboards in Wisconsin have a hard life. Years of sun through a windscreen dries the surface out, and once it goes chalky and starts to craze, no product reverses it. Treatment slows that considerably, but only if it is done before the damage rather than after.

We finish matte rather than glossy on purpose. A glossy dash reflects into the windscreen and is genuinely distracting at low sun angles. If you want the wet-look finish, say so and we will do it — but we will not do it by default.

Glass, and the film nobody talks about

Interior glass is the other tell of whether a detail was done properly. Windscreens develop a film from plastic off-gassing combined with road dust and, in older vehicles, years of accumulated residue.

That film is why oncoming headlights scatter and dazzle at night, and most people have adapted to it so gradually that they no longer notice it is there. It is also why a single pass with a household glass spray usually makes things worse — it dissolves the film and redistributes it into streaks instead of removing it.

We do interior glass in two stages for that reason. It is a small thing that changes night driving noticeably.

What this service does not include

Spot stain treatment is included — a coffee ring, a scuff on a door card, a patch on a seat base. Widespread staining across multiple surfaces is a bigger job and we will quote it honestly rather than pretend it fits.

Deep odor work is also separate. If a vehicle smells of smoke, pets, or mildew from a leak, the smell has a source and cleaning the interior does not necessarily remove it. That is odor removal, and it is a different process.

If you want the exterior done as well, the full detail covers both and costs less than booking the two separately.
