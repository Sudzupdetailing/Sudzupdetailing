Interior Protection

Everything else we do is repair. This is the one service that works ahead of the damage instead of after it — and the damage it prevents is largely the kind you cannot undo.

Prevention is the only real fix for some of this

Most interior damage can be improved after the fact. Some cannot, and that is the category this service exists for.

A dashboard that has gone chalky and started to craze from years of UV is not recoverable. Leather that has dried out and cracked at the bolster is not recoverable. A light-coloured fabric seat that took a red drink and sat for a week is often permanently marked. Cleaning helps all three look better; none of them go back to what they were.

Protection is cheap relative to what it prevents, and it is genuinely boring — you never see it work. The evidence is a four-year-old interior that still looks like a two-year-old one.

Fabric sealant, and what it actually does

A fabric sealant bonds to individual fibres and changes their surface energy, so liquid beads on top of the weave instead of immediately wicking down into the backing.

That does not make a seat spill-proof and we will not tell you it does. What it does is buy time. An untreated cloth seat takes a spilled drink into the foam within seconds, at which point extraction is the only option and complete removal is not guaranteed. A treated seat holds it on the surface long enough that a towel handles it. That difference is the whole product.

It also slows the daily grind — body oils, food residue and general soiling working into the fibre — which is what makes a driver's seat look tired years before the rest of the interior does.

Leather and vinyl coating

Automotive leather is almost always coated rather than raw. There is a polymer finish over the hide, and that finish is what fails — not the leather beneath it. Once it cracks, you are looking at repair or replacement.

What kills it is a combination of UV, heat and abrasion, concentrated almost entirely on the driver's outer bolster, which takes the friction of getting in and out twice a day, and on whichever panels get direct sun.

A protective coating adds a sacrificial layer that takes the abrasion and blocks a meaningful amount of UV. It also keeps the surface from drying, which is what makes leather stiff before it cracks. We finish these matte for the same reason we finish dashboards matte — glare off a glossy seat or dash is genuinely distracting at low sun angles.

Why July matters here more than people think

Interiors take their worst damage in high summer, and almost nobody treats it as a seasonal problem the way they treat winter salt.

Sustained sun through a windscreen dries plasticiser out of dash plastics and moisture out of leather. A parked car in July builds cabin temperatures far above ambient, and that heat accelerates every one of those processes. A vehicle that sits in a work car park all day, every day, through a Wisconsin summer is taking real cumulative damage while nothing visible happens.

The practical implication is that protection is worth applying in spring or early summer rather than autumn. Applied in October it spends its first five months protecting against a season that was never the threat.

How this fits with everything else

Protection is always applied to a freshly cleaned surface, never over an existing one. Sealing a dirty seat locks the soiling into the fibre and makes it harder to remove later, so this is booked alongside an interior detail or a full detail rather than on its own.

It is worth most on vehicles that are about to face the hardest conditions rather than ones that already have. A new or newly bought car, a family vehicle carrying kids and food daily, a work truck, a light-coloured interior, or a car with a long daily commute in the sun.

It is worth least on an interior that is already damaged. Protecting a cracked dash preserves the crack. We will tell you if that is what we are looking at.
