Paint Correction

Correction removes defects from your clear coat rather than filling or hiding them. It is the only process that actually fixes swirl marks, and it is finite — which is why it has to be done carefully.

What swirl marks actually are

Swirl marks are thousands of fine scratches in the clear coat, each one too small to see individually. You notice them as a spiderweb or halo pattern around a point light source — direct sun, a streetlight, a torch held close to the panel.

They are damage, not dirt. Nothing you wash the car with will remove them, because there is nothing sitting on the surface to remove. The surface itself has been cut.

The overwhelming majority come from wash technique rather than from the road. Automatic brush washes drag whatever the previous vehicle left across your paint. Drying with a towel that has picked up grit does it. Washing with one bucket and reloading a dirty mitt does it. Years of small, well-intentioned damage accumulates into a finish that looks permanently dull because light scatters instead of reflecting.

How correction works, and why it is finite

The clear coat on your car is a layer of lacquer over the colour, typically somewhere in the region of forty to sixty microns on a modern factory finish — thinner than a sheet of paper.

Correction works by levelling. A machine polisher with an abrasive compound removes a controlled few microns of clear coat, taking the surface down to the base of the scratches so what remains is flat and reflects light uniformly. The scratch is not filled. The surface around it is lowered to meet it.

Which is why this is a finite resource. Every correction consumes clear coat, and you cannot put it back. A car can typically take a handful of proper corrections in its life. This is the entire reason we assess before quoting and the reason we will sometimes recommend doing less than you asked for. A shop that will happily do aggressive correction on a car that has already had it twice is selling you a problem you will not see until the clear coat fails.

Assessment: the part that determines everything

Correction cannot be quoted from a photograph or a phone call, and any quote given that way is a guess.

We inspect under focused light, which is the only way to see the true defect pattern — daylight hides most of it and a bright showroom hides the rest. What we are looking for is what kind of defects are present, how deep they run, whether the clear coat has enough left to work with, and whether previous correction has already been done.

Some defects will not come out. If a scratch catches your fingernail, it is usually through the clear coat, and levelling to its base would remove more clear than is safe. Deep stone chips are through to primer or metal. We will tell you which of your defects are coming out and which are not, before you commit to anything.

One stage or multiple

A single-stage correction uses one compound and pad combination to remove the majority of lighter defects. It typically recovers most of the gloss for a fraction of the time and cost, and for a daily-driven car it is very often the right answer.

Multi-stage means cutting more aggressively first, then refining with progressively finer steps to remove the haze that the cutting stage leaves behind. It gets closer to a genuinely defect-free finish and it takes substantially longer.

The honest framing is diminishing returns. Going from neglected to noticeably better is fast. Going from very good to nearly perfect takes several times as long as everything before it and consumes more clear coat. Which one is right depends on the car, how it is used and what you actually want from it — and that is a conversation, not a package.

Correction and coating belong together

A ceramic coating is clear and it seals whatever is underneath it. Coating a swirled car locks the swirls in for the life of the coating, and removing them later means removing the coating first.

So if you are considering both, correction comes first, always, and they are done as one job. That sequence is not upselling — it is the only order that makes sense, and doing it the other way round wastes the coating.

The paint also has to be fully decontaminated before correction begins, since polishing over bonded contamination drags it across the panel. That work is covered on the exterior detailing page and is part of any correction job.
