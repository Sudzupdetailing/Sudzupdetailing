Freight film is greasy, not gritty

Sharing Highway 26 with heavy freight puts a specific layer onto a vehicle: diesel soot, tyre rubber, oil and road water, thrown up as a continuous mist by every truck in wet conditions. It is oily. It holds dirt. It bonds as it dries, and it builds in layers over years.

That layer is why a Watertown car can be washed weekly and still look flat, and why the windscreen smears under the wipers rather than clearing. The soot is in the film on the glass as much as on the paint.

The exterior half of a full detail on these starts with a degreasing stage that most vehicles do not need, then iron removal for the brake and rail component, then clay. Then protection, because bare paint back on the corridor picks the film up again within weeks.

The cabin on a corridor commuter

Watertown commuting runs west toward Madison on I-94 and east toward the county on Highway 16, and a corridor commuter's cabin has the concentrated driver's-quarter wear that comes with real daily mileage. Glass on the inside carries the same soot film as the outside, from the cabin intake pulling corridor air, and it is the reason night driving has become harder without the owner quite noticing.

Two-stage interior glass cleaning is standard on every vehicle we do. On a Watertown vehicle it makes the largest difference of any single step.

Making the day worth it

Thirty-five minutes each way is a real trip, and a full detail is a full-day drop-off. It makes sense for a vehicle that has done years on the corridor without a reset, and it does not make sense for a wash. We will be straight about which you have.

April is the month, after the salt stops. September is the alternative if protection before winter is the priority.
