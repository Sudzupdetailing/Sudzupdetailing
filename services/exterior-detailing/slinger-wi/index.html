What comes off a quarter-mile oval

A high-banked asphalt oval under racing load shreds tyre rubber continuously, and the particles are small enough to stay airborne for hours. On race nights that plume drifts across the paddock, the lots and the surrounding streets, and settles on every vehicle parked within range.

Rubber dust is not mineral and not organic. It is a polymer, it is slightly oily from the compounds in racing tyres, and when it lands on warm paint it softens and adheres rather than sitting loose. It also carries the asphalt fines the tyres lift off the track surface, so what arrives on a Slinger bonnet after a race night is a dark, faintly greasy film bonded to the clear coat.

Why it survives washing

Water does not touch it — rubber is hydrophobic by design. Detergent lifts the asphalt fines and leaves the rubber. So a race-night car that gets washed the next day comes out cleaner and still has a dark haze across the leading surfaces that the owner cannot account for.

The film also picks up everything that lands on it afterwards. Ordinary dust sticks to the oily rubber layer, so a Slinger car that has sat through a season of race nights carries a composite film that is thicker and darker than the mileage would suggest.

Removing it, and the glass

Rubber film needs a solvent stage — the same chemistry that handles road tar — before the standard iron and clay decontamination. Skip it and the clay drags through the rubber rather than lifting it.

Glass takes it worst, because rubber film on a windscreen smears under wipers rather than clearing, and the dark haze scatters oncoming light. Two-stage glass cleaning with a solvent first is standard on any Slinger vehicle that lives near the track.

Then protection, because a coated surface does not give softened rubber anything to adhere to. On a vehicle that parks near the Speedway through the season, a coating turns race-night dust from a bonding problem into a rinsing one.
