Owner Guide

How Often Should You Detail Your Car in Wisconsin?

The honest answer is that it depends on how you use the vehicle, not on a fixed interval. Here is a realistic schedule for a few common situations.

The one universal recommendation

If you take nothing else from this: get one full interior and exterior detail after salt season ends, in late March or early April.

That single appointment does more than any other timing. It removes salt from the carpet before it spends a summer grinding into the fibres, clears brine from the jambs and arches before warm humid weather accelerates corrosion, and puts protection back on paint before the strongest UV months.

If you detail your car once a year and nothing else, do it then. Not in December when everything is about to get salted again, and not in July when the damage is already done.

The daily commuter

A vehicle doing an hour a day on US 41 or 45 accumulates road film on the outside and driver's-side wear on the inside faster than almost anything else.

A reasonable schedule is a full detail in spring, an interior service around September, and regular washing in between with attention to the lower panels. The September interior catches the summer accumulation before winter closes the car up for five months, which is when smells develop.

The one thing worth doing more often is the dash and upper door cards. Commuter vehicles get consistent direct sun and those plastics dry out. VRP treatment two or three times a year meaningfully slows that.

The family vehicle

Family SUVs and minivans need interior attention more often than anything else, and it has nothing to do with mileage.

Realistically, an interior service every four to six months. The driver here is organic material — food, drinks, wet clothing — which does not sit inert like road dust. It decomposes, and the longer it sits the more likely you move from a cleaning problem to an odour problem.

If something spills badly, do not wait for the next scheduled visit. A fresh spill treated within days usually comes out completely. The same spill six months later may be a permanent shadow and a lasting smell.

The recreation vehicle

If your vehicle carries dogs, bikes, skis, golf clubs or lake gear, work to the season rather than the calendar.

The pattern that works around here is one detail at the end of the winter sports season in April, and one at the end of the summer lake and trail season in September or October. Each catches a complete cycle of a specific kind of contamination at the point it has finished, rather than mid-cycle when it is only going to build up again.

Sand is the exception that justifies an out-of-schedule visit. Sand is abrasive and it sands your upholstery every time you sit down. If you have had a genuinely sandy summer, do not leave it in there until October.

The low-mileage or garaged car

A vehicle that does five thousand miles a year and lives in a garage needs less, but not nothing.

Once a year is usually sufficient, and spring is still the right time. The thing to watch on low-mileage cars is not dirt but drying: dash plastics and rubber seals degrade with time and UV regardless of how far the car has driven.

Cars that sit also develop stale interiors, because moisture never gets driven out by regular use and heat cycles. If a rarely-used car smells musty, that is usually the reason.

A note on over-detailing

It is possible to detail too often, and any detailer telling you otherwise is selling appointments.

Interior cleaning is essentially never harmful. Repeated aggressive exterior polishing is a different matter — polishing removes a microscopic layer of clear coat every time, and a car polished every month for years will eventually have measurably less clear coat than one polished twice a year.

For the finishing polish in a normal detail this is not a practical concern at sensible intervals. But it is a real reason not to book paint work more often than the vehicle needs, and a reason to be sceptical of anyone recommending monthly polishing.
