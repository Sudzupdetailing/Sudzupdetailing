Lease Return Inspection: What Gets Charged

A lease inspector is not a buyer. They work from a checklist with dollar values on it, and the whole game is taking items off that list before they see the car.

How the inspection works

Lease and fleet returns are inspected against the lessor's wear-and-tear guidelines, which are usually published and worth reading before the return. Most work on thresholds: damage below a stated size or severity is normal wear and not charged; above it, there is a charge per item.

The inspector is not forming an impression. They are checking items against thresholds and photographing what exceeds them. That is a fundamentally different standard from a private sale, where a clean, good-smelling car with a couple of flaws sells fine. On a return, the flaws are individually billed and the clean is irrelevant.

So the useful question is not whether the car looks good. It is which specific items on the guideline your car currently fails, and which of those can be fixed for less than the charge.

What is typically charged

Odor is almost universally charged, frequently as a flat fee regardless of cause, and it is one of the highest-value items to address because it is also one of the most fixable.

Interior stains above a size threshold. Upholstery burns, tears and cuts. Excessive wear on the driver's seat. Scuffed or gouged door sills and kick panels. Dash and trim damage. Missing or damaged floor mats.

Exterior: paint scratches and scuffs above a length threshold, dents above a size threshold, chips in clusters, curb rash on wheels, damaged glass, and mismatched or poorly repaired paint. Many guidelines also charge for paint condition generally — heavy swirl marring or oxidation — though this varies.

Tyres below a tread threshold and non-matching tyres. Missing equipment. Aftermarket modifications not returned to stock.

What detailing fixes, and what it does not

Fixable: odor, if the source is found and removed rather than masked. Surface stains on cloth and light leather, provided they are not dye transfer. Sill and kick-panel scuffing, which usually cleans up well. Dash haze. Light wash marring on paint, with correction. General presentation, which does not remove charged items but does affect how carefully the inspector looks.

Not fixable by detailing, and we will not pretend otherwise: scratches through the clear coat, dents, chips to primer, torn or burned upholstery, cracked dash plastics, curb-rashed wheels, glass damage, tyres. Those are repair items and some of them are worth repairing before return; a paintless dent removal or a wheel refurb often costs less than the charge. But they are not detailing.

The honest value of a return prep is that we go through the guideline with the car in front of us and tell you which items fall in each category, before you spend anything.

When to book

Weeks before the return date, not days. If something needs a second pass — odor work often does — you want the time to do it. If the assessment turns up repair items, you want time to get them done.

Get the guideline from the lessor first. Every lessor's thresholds are slightly different and the useful conversation is about your specific document.

And photograph the car thoroughly on the day you return it, from every angle, inside and out, with the odometer visible. Disputes happen, and the photographs are the only thing that settles them.
