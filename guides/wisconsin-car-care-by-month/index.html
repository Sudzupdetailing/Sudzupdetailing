Car Care Through a Wisconsin Year

Almost every car care schedule you find online was written for somewhere that does not have our winter, our lakes, or our harvest. This one is written for Washington County, month by month, from a shop on WI-83 that sees what each season actually does to a vehicle.

Why a Wisconsin calendar looks nothing like a national one

The standard advice is to wash your car every two weeks and detail it twice a year. That is fine guidance for a mild climate. It is close to useless here, because in Wisconsin the calendar is not evenly spaced. There are months where washing a car accomplishes almost nothing, and there are two or three narrow windows where the same work pays for itself several times over.

There is also a piece of local structure most drivers do not know about. WisDOT does not actually maintain its own roads in winter. It contracts the work out to the county highway departments, which means the salt and brine on WI-83, Highway 60 and the county trunks around Hartford are applied on Washington County's schedule, by Washington County crews. When that schedule starts and stops is the single biggest factor in what happens to your vehicle each year.

Washington County was one of ten counties used in a UW–Madison Traffic Operations and Safety Lab field study of salt brine, so the way our roads get treated has been measured more closely than most. Brine is roughly a 23 percent salt solution, sprayed as a liquid, and crews use it for anti-icing — meaning it goes down ahead of a storm rather than after one. That single detail changes the whole year, and it is why this calendar starts in October rather than January.

October — brine season starts before winter does

This is the month drivers get caught out. The first brine runs go down before the first snow, because that is the entire point of anti-icing: put a salt layer on dry pavement so ice cannot bond to it. Your car picks up salt on a clear, dry, fifty-degree October afternoon when washing it is the last thing on your mind.

Brine dries into a thin white film and wicks into exactly the places rinse water does not reach — door jambs, rocker panels, the seam where carpet meets the sill plate. Because there is no slush and no visible spray, nothing about the drive tells you it happened.

October is also the last good window for exterior protection. Whatever is on your paint in late October is what will be carrying you through five months of salt. If you are considering ceramic coating, this is close to the last month temperatures reliably support a proper cure.

November — the last honest look at your paint

By mid-November the roads are being treated in earnest and your car will not be genuinely clean again until spring. Before that happens, it is worth getting the paint decontaminated and protected once, because everything you do after this is damage control.

This is also collector and seasonal vehicle month. If a car is going into storage, wash it first — properly, including the underside. Salt film left on paint and steel for five stationary months in an unheated Wisconsin garage does more corrosion damage than the same car would have taken being driven. A stored car with salt on it is not resting; it is quietly corroding in the dark.

December — the interior takes the beating

December is when interiors get destroyed, and it has almost nothing to do with weather. Holiday travel, more passengers than usual, food in the car, gifts and bags in the cargo area, and boots that have been through a parking lot four times a day.

The mechanical problem underneath it is short trips. In cold weather a five or ten minute drive never gets the cabin fully warm, so the moisture your boots brought in never evaporates. It sits in the carpet backing. Repeat that daily for three weeks and you have built the exact conditions that produce the musty smell people notice in February and blame on something else.

January — the month to work on the inside, not the outside

In deep cold, exterior washing gets you very little. Rock salt loses most of its effectiveness below roughly fifteen degrees, so crews shift to different blends and sand, and whatever you wash off is back within a day of driving. Doors freeze shut, water sits in seals, and you have spent money for a car that looks clean for the length of one commute.

The interior is a completely different story. January is when salt is actively sitting in your carpet and wicking with every thaw, and when that trapped December moisture is at its worst. It is the best month of the year for interior detailing, and honestly it is our quietest, so it is the easiest month to get a booking.

It is also the month people first notice odor. If a vehicle smells and you cannot find the source, it is usually not the source you are imagining — it is moisture and salt in the carpet backing, and it needs extraction rather than a spray.

February — take the thaw window

Wisconsin almost always gives up a stretch of days above freezing in February. That window is the one genuinely worthwhile mid-winter exterior wash, and the priority is not the paint. It is the underside, the wheel wells, the door jambs and the rocker panels, where five weeks of accumulated brine film is sitting.

The reason to do it during a thaw rather than on a cold clear day is drainage. Above freezing, water actually runs out of the seams and jambs instead of freezing inside them. Washing a car at twenty degrees pushes water into places that then expand as ice, which is how door seals get torn and lock mechanisms get stiff.

March — the worst month, and the one people misjudge

March punishes the impatient. The first warm week arrives, everybody books a full detail, and then the county treats the roads twice more in the last week of the month and the car is back to where it started.

March is also mechanically the hardest month on a vehicle. Freeze-thaw cycling means the salt on your car dissolves, wicks deeper, and recrystallises repeatedly, which is far more corrosive than salt that stays dry. Road spray is at its filthiest.

Our honest advice, and we say this knowing it costs us March bookings: wait. If you want one full detail this year, it is worth more to you in April.

April — the real reset

This is the month the calendar turns. Once the county is done treating roads for the season, the salt on your vehicle is the last salt of the year, and removing it actually sticks. April is comfortably the highest-value month on this calendar.

The work that matters now is decontamination rather than washing. Five months of brine, brake dust and road film leave contamination chemically bonded to paint that soap will not touch — that needs an iron remover and a clay stage, which is what separates a real exterior service from a wash. On the inside, carpet needs hot water extraction to pull salt out of the backing, not a surface vacuum that skates over it.

April is also when winter paint damage becomes visible for the first time. Sun angle and clean paint together reveal the swirls and marring that were hidden under grime since November. If you are going to book paint correction, this is when you will first see why you need it.

May — lake flies, pollen and a very specific mess

If you are anywhere in our northern service area, mid-May brings the Lake Winnebago lake fly hatch. It arrives around Mother's Day most years, runs roughly two weeks, and the concentration on any given day depends entirely on wind direction. Drivers around Fond du Lac get the worst of it.

The problem for paint is not the insects themselves but what they leave — a green residue that has to be washed off rather than wiped, and that bakes on fast in May sun. Left for a few warm days it etches. If you commute north during the hatch, wash promptly rather than waiting for it to finish.

May is also peak tree pollen. Pollen is mildly acidic and it collects in cowl vents and window seals, then washes down onto paint with the first rain. This is the month people run their cabin air and discover the smell is coming from the vents.

June — the best month for correction work

June is the technical high point of the year. Long daylight, stable moderate temperatures and low humidity are close to ideal conditions for polishing and for curing coatings. If you are doing one serious paint job this year, June and September are the two months that make it easiest to do properly.

June is also when Pike Lake starts showing up in vehicles. The state park unit is minutes from our shop and from June onward we pull a genuinely surprising amount of sand out of local cars. Wet sand tracked onto carpet dries into a crust that a household vacuum will pass straight over without lifting.

July — protect the interior, not the paint

July is the hardest month of the year on interiors, and almost nobody treats it that way. Sustained sun through a windscreen dries plasticiser out of dash plastics, and once a dash has gone chalky and started to craze, no product reverses it. Leather and vinyl lose moisture the same way.

This is the month interior protection earns its cost, because it is preventative and the damage it prevents is permanent. Exterior work in July is harder, not easier — hot panels flash-dry soap and water before you can remove them, which is how you get water spotting in the middle of summer.

Bird droppings and tree sap are also at their worst now, and both are genuinely urgent. Dropping etching is acid damage; on hot paint it can mark clear coat in a single afternoon. Get it off the same day.

August — a second hatch and the last of summer damage

There is a second, smaller lake fly hatch on the Winnebago system in August. It catches people out because they associate the hatch with spring and have stopped watching for it.

August is also when ragweed starts and when a summer of accumulated sap, dropping etch and sun exposure has done whatever it is going to do. It is a good month to have the paint assessed honestly, because whatever correction work you want done before winter needs to happen in the next six weeks.

September — the ceramic coating month

If there is one month to book ceramic coating, this is it. Temperatures and humidity still support a proper cure, the summer's damage can be corrected first, and the coating goes on with a full winter of brine ahead of it — which is exactly the season it earns its money.

September also brings harvest. Washington County is still working farmland, and corn and soybean harvest throws fine dust across the rural stretches of WI-83, Highway 60 and the county trunks. That dust settles into dash vents and seat stitching, where vacuuming alone does not reach it, and it needs compressed air and brushwork before it gets driven deeper into the foam.

And then the cycle closes. In four to six weeks the first brine trucks go out again, whether or not it has snowed.

How to actually use this

If you only do one thing a year, do a full interior and exterior detail in April. Nothing else on this calendar returns as much.

If you do two, add September, and use it for protection ahead of winter rather than cleaning after summer.

If you do three, add January — interior only. It is the month the inside of the car is at its worst, the month the work lasts longest, and the month we can most easily fit you in.

Everything else here is situational. Wash after the lake fly hatch. Take the February thaw. Do not book in March. Get droppings off the same day in July. None of that requires a detailer, and most of it we would rather you just knew.
